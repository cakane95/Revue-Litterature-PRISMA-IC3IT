\begin{abstract}
The sustainable management of Socio-Ecological Systems (SES), such as agro-sylvo-pastoral systems (ASPS) in the Sahel, requires adaptive decision-making capable of balancing micro-level objectives (e.g., farmer production targets) with macro-level long-term goals (e.g., environmental sustainability). The coupling of Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) and Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) presents a promising paradigm for designing prescriptive control in these environments. Yet, "Full MARL" approaches face critical barriers to practical, human-centered, real-world applications, including computational cost, state-space explosion, and scalability challenges. This paper presents a systematic review, based on the PRISMA protocol, mapping the state-of-the-art of MARL applications in decentralized resource management during the Deep MARL era (2017–present). Our synthesis of the resulting 75-article corpus reveals a research gap defined by two key findings: (1) a limited number of contributions focused on non-urban SES applications (e.g., agriculture), and (2) a pronounced reliance on non-hierarchical architectures (84\% of articles), which may help explain this gap. Based on this analysis, we argue that Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), which combines high-level strategic agents with lower-level agents for instantaneous execution, emerges as a fruitful architectural research direction. HRL offers a potential pathway toward achieving robust, scalable, and computationally tractable decentralized control in complex SES.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{introduction}

The sustainable management of complex Socio-Ecological Systems (SES), characterized by non-linear dynamics, actor heterogeneity, and uncertainty, represents a fundamental scientific and societal challenge \cite{gottsAgentbasedModellingSocioecological2019}. Designing adaptive and resilient interventions in such environments requires modeling paradigms capable of capturing decentralized interactions and the emergence of collective behaviors. The Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) paradigm effectively represents these bottom-up processes \cite{Zhang20232170}. However, traditional ABMs rely on fixed behavioral rules, limiting their capacity to model adaptive, goal-oriented decision-making required for dynamic resource management \cite{kaneEcoSysMLMetamodelSocioecological2025}. To overcome this limitation, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) provides a framework through which agents can learn optimal policies by interacting with their environment \cite{littmanMarkovGamesFramework1994, sertSegregationDynamicsReinforcement2020}.

Despite this promise, the integration of ABM and MARL faces critical barriers related to scalability and computational tractability. A “full MARL” approach, where multiple learning agents interact concurrently, drastically increases the state–action space dimensionality \cite{zhangMultiAgentReinforcementLearning2021}, which makes real-world deployment difficult. Hierarchical architectures offer a potential pathway to mitigate this complexity, yet recent evidence indicates that their adoption remains limited. In addition, applications in non-urban SES domains, such as agriculture, forestry, or water management, remain scarce even though these systems are inherently multi-scale. 

Compared to existing reviews, such as \cite{gautronReinforcementLearningCrop2022}, which focuses on RL for crop management, and \cite{Zhang20232170}, which surveys broader ML–ABM synergies, our study specifically targets the methodological intersection of ABM and MARL for decentralized resource management. We go beyond descriptive overviews by quantifying two key gaps: an architectural gap between hierarchical and non-hierarchical MARL, and a domain gap reflecting the limited use of these methods in non-urban SES. By mapping these dual gaps, this review provides a methodological perspective on the readiness of MARL–ABM frameworks for real-world, data-scarce socio-ecological contexts such as the Sahel.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{motivation} presents the motivation and objectives. Section~\ref{background} introduces the theoretical foundations of sequential decision-making. Section~\ref{methodology} details the systematic review methodology. Section~\ref{results} analyzes the corpus. Section~\ref{research_directions} discusses future research directions. Finally, Section~\ref{conclusion} concludes.

\section{Motivation \& Objectives}
\label{motivation}

The Sahelian crisis exemplifies the challenges of managing complex SES \cite{vermeulenClimateChangeFood2012}. Its fragile agro-sylvo-pastoral systems (ASPS) are driven by complex interactions between crops, livestock, trees, and climate \cite{traoreAgentBasedModelTranshumant2024}. Traditional top-down interventions have often failed by neglecting these territorial dynamics and heterogeneous actor decisions \cite{turnerEnvironmentalRehabilitationVulnerability2021, mirzabaevEconomicEfficiencyTargeting2022}, underscoring the need for an integrated, bottom-up modeling paradigm \cite{gaudouMAELIAMultiagentPlatform2013}.

While the ABM-MARL coupling (Section \ref{introduction}) offers a promising framework for designing adaptive policies in such environments, its real-world effectiveness is constrained by scalability and computational cost, particularly in data-poor contexts.

In recent years, RL has been increasingly adopted in agriculture to support farm-level decision-making and process automation. Several studies have explored constrained RL formulations to ensure agronomic feasibility and interpretability. For example, \cite{goldenitsTabularReinforcementLearning2025} developed explainable tabular RL for crop rotation planning, introducing reward perturbations to enhance robustness and trustworthiness. Similarly, \cite{bajaAdaptiveFertilizerManagement2025} applied constrained RL to optimize nitrogen use efficiency under agronomic and environmental constraints, demonstrating the ability of RL agents to learn adaptive fertilization policies. Beyond crop management, RL has also guided robotic control and automation, such as collision-free peduncle grasping for tomato harvesting using deep RL with posture constraints \cite{liPeduncleCollisionfreeGrasping2024}. At a larger operational scale, MARL has been employed to coordinate fleets of agricultural machines for sugarcane bale logistics under equipment breakdown constraints \cite{pitakasoOptimizingSugarcaneBale2025}.

Although these studies illustrate the growing sophistication of RL and MARL in agriculture, they primarily address equipment control or field-scale optimization. At the time of writing this article, we have not identified studies that explicitly model socially heterogeneous agents (e.g. farmers, regulators, etc..) or capture the territorial interactions and adaptive governance processes that characterize real socio-ecological systems. This scientific gap motivates the present review, which systematically maps how coupling ABM and MARL can advance decentralized, learning-driven management in agricultural and environmental contexts.

Therefore, the primary objectives of \textit{this paper} are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To systematically identify and map the current state-of-the-art in coupling ABM and MARL for Socio-Ecological Systems.
    \item To critically analyze the \textit{architectures} of these models, with a specific focus on identifying hybrid or hierarchical structures that mitigate complexity.
    \item To identify the key research gaps, particularly regarding model scalability and application in data-poor contexts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background : Sequential Decision-Making}
\label{background}

At the heart of managing complex systems, such as farming in the Sahel, lies the problem of \textbf{sequential decision-making}. Each growing season, a farmer must decide when and what to plant, how to fertilize, and when to harvest to achieve long-term production goals. These trial-and-error decisions illustrate two central challenges: delayed feedback, since outcomes appear much later, and the combinatorial explosion of possible actions for a given objective.

\subsection{Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)}

This problem is formally represented by a \textbf{Markov Decision Process (MDP)} \cite{putermanMarkovDecisionProcesses2005}, based on the \textbf{Markov Property}, which states that given the current state, the future is independent of past states and actions.

An MDP is defined as a quadruple $\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R} \rangle$ \cite{suttonReinforcementLearningIntroduction2014}, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of possible \textbf{states}, $\mathcal{A}$ the set of \textbf{actions}, $\mathcal{P}(s'|s,a)$ the \textbf{transition function} describing the probability of moving from $s$ to $s'$ after action $a$, and $\mathcal{R}(s,a,s')$ the \textbf{reward function}, the scalar feedback received after that transition.

The agent's objective is to learn a behavior, or \textbf{policy}, that maximizes a cumulative objective function (generally the expected discounted cumulative reward or \textbf{return}). Solving the MDP, therefore, involves finding an \textbf{optimal policy} $\pi^{*}(a|s)$ that yields the highest possible expected return from any starting state.

The fundamental questions of "How do we compare policies?" and "How do we find an optimal policy?" are addressed by the \textbf{Value function} ($V^{\pi}$) and the \textbf{Quality function} ($Q^{\pi}$) \cite{suttonReinforcementLearningIntroduction2014}. The recursive \textbf{Bellman Equations} provide the formal basis for these functions and enable iterative solution methods.

\textbf{Value Function ($V^{\pi}$) :}
\begin{equation}
V^{\pi}(s) = \sum_{a} \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s',r} p(s',r \mid s,a)\,\bigl[r + \gamma \, V^{\pi}(s')\bigr].
\label{eq:V_pi}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Quality Function ($Q^{\pi}$) :}
\begin{equation}
Q^{\pi}(s, a) = \mathcal{R}(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} \mathcal{P}(s'|s, a) \sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a'|s') Q^{\pi}(s', a')
\label{eq:Q_pi}
\end{equation}

\noindent The optimal policy $\pi^{*}$ is found by maximizing the optimal action-value function ($Q^{*}$):
\begin{equation}
\pi^{*}(s) = \arg \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} Q^{*}(s, a)
\label{eq:pi_star}
\end{equation}

As shown, the MDP provides a robust framework to represent the sequential decision-making problem. However, its core assumption of \textbf{full observability} of the system is often violated in real-world scenarios.

\subsection{Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) and Reinforcement Learning (RL)}

Consider a farmer in Senegal who must plant millet shortly before the first major rains. The true state of the system ($S$), such as the exact timing of rainfall, is unobservable. Instead, the farmer relies on partial \textbf{observations} ($o$) like forecasts or local knowledge to estimate environmental conditions.

A \textbf{Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)} extends the MDP framework by adding an observation set $\Omega$ and an observation function $\mathcal{O}$ \cite{kaelblingPlanningActingPartially1998}. Because the agent cannot act on the true state $S \in \mathcal{S}$, it maintains a \textbf{belief state} $b(s)$, a probability distribution representing its uncertainty given past observations. Both MDPs and POMDPs can be solved through \textbf{Reinforcement Learning (RL)}, where an agent learns an optimal policy via \textit{trial-and-error} interaction with its environment \cite{suttonReinforcementLearningIntroduction2014}.

Ref.~\cite{gautronReinforcementLearningCrop2022} classifies modern RL algorithms into three archetypal families: \textbf{Planning} methods, which derive a policy by interacting with a simulator of the true environment, such as heuristic search and Monte Carlo Tree Search \cite{suttonReinforcementLearningIntroduction2014, kocsisImprovedMontecarloSearch}; \textbf{Critic} methods, which learn a value function ($V$ or $Q$) as in Q-Learning or SARSA \cite{watkinsQlearning1992, suttonReinforcementLearningIntroduction2014}; and \textbf{Actor} methods, which directly learn an optimal policy ($\pi$) without an explicit value function, as in REINFORCE \cite{williamsSimpleStatisticalGradientfollowing1992}.

Modern approaches often combine these archetypes, most notably through the Actor–Critic architecture, which simultaneously learns a policy (Actor) and a value function (Critic). Prominent examples include Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) \cite{haarnojaSoftActorCriticOffPolicy2018, schulmanProximalPolicyOptimization2017}. When implemented with deep neural networks as function approximators, these methods define the field of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). The AlphaGo Zero program \cite{silverMasteringGameGo2017}, which mastered the game of Go “without human knowledge” by combining all three archetypes, is widely recognized as the milestone that ushered in the modern DRL era.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)}

Extending the sequential decision problem to multiple interacting entities leads to MARL. For instance, if a group of neighboring farmers in the same community must cooperatively decide which parcels of land to leave fallow each season for animal parking (critical for carbon and nutrient cycling), the success of the collective carbon sequestration goal depends on the coordinated actions of all. This scenario is formalized as a \textbf{Stochastic Game} (or \textbf{Markov Game}) \cite{littmanMarkovGamesFramework1994}.

MARL faces two fundamental challenges: non-stationarity, since each agent learns in an environment that evolves with the changing policies of other agents, and credit assignment, the difficulty of linking a collective reward ($R_{\text{total}}$) to individual actions (e.g., leaving one’s parcel fallow) \cite{perolatMultiagentReinforcementLearning2017}.

The dominant framework to address these challenges is \textbf{Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)} \cite{loweMultiAgentActorCriticMixed2020}. CTDE mitigates non-stationarity by using a global Critic during training with joint $(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a})$, while decentralized Actors rely on local observations $(o_i)$ for scalable deployment. Major CTDE variants include \textbf{Value Decomposition} methods (e.g., QMIX \cite{rashidQMIXMonotonicValue2018a}), which tackle credit assignment by factorizing $Q_{\text{tot}}$ into agent-specific $Q_i$, and \textbf{Policy Gradient Extensions} (e.g., MAPPO \cite{yuSurprisingEffectivenessPPO2022}), which leverage a centralized Critic to guide multiple Actors.

Although CTDE ensures coordination, it struggles with the sparse and delayed rewards typical of resource management (e.g., seasonal harvest yield). \textbf{Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL)} addresses this long-horizon issue by decomposing tasks across multiple timescales \cite{suttonMDPsSemiMDPsFramework1999}. In paradigms such as Feudal Reinforcement Learning (FRL) \cite{dayanFeudalReinforcementLearning1992}, a high-level \textbf{Manager} sets abstract goals (e.g., maximize carbon sequestration), while a low-level \textbf{Worker} executes primitive actions (e.g., apply organic fertilizer). The Worker receives intrinsic rewards for achieving subgoals, thereby alleviating the temporal credit assignment problem.

Beyond HRL, complexity is mitigated by hybrid MARL frameworks that integrate RL with structural techniques. These include using \textbf{Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)} to encode spatial dependencies (e.g., node connectivity) into the state representation \cite{10.1145/3580305.3599839}, or embedding MARL into \textbf{Agent-Based Models (ABMs)} to replace fixed behavioral rules with adaptive, learned policies \cite{sertSegregationDynamicsReinforcement2020}.

Building on this foundation, we now present our systematic review methodology.

\section{Methodology}
\label{methodology}

This review follows the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines \cite{haddawayPRISMA2020PackageShiny2022}. A multi-faceted search strategy, targeting both fundamental computer science and domain-specific literature, was executed across Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and HAL using three query streams:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core Query (Q1):} Targets the direct intersection of the two technologies. \\
    \textit{(("multi-agent reinforcement learning" OR "MARL") AND ("agent-based model" OR "ABM" OR "multi-agent system"))}

    \item \textbf{Thematic Query (Q2):} Refines Q1 with socio-ecological application terms to be run on generalist databases. \\
    \textit{(Q1) AND ("socio-ecological system" OR "environmental management" OR "natural resource management" OR "sustainability" OR "common-pool resource" OR "carbon" OR "land use" OR "agriculture" OR "forestry")}

    \item \textbf{Architectural Query (Q3):} Identifies works on hierarchical control architectures, run on specialist databases. \\
    \textit{(("hierarchical reinforcement learning" OR "governing agent" OR "macro-micro" OR "CTDE" OR "centralized training decentralized execution") AND ("agent-based model" OR "multi-agent system"))}
\end{itemize}

All searches were filtered by: \textbf{Time} (2017–Present, marking the publication of key Deep MARL algorithms like MADDPG); \textbf{Language} (English, French); and \textbf{Document Type} (Conference Papers, Proceedings, Journal Articles, Reviews).

To filter the initial search pool, we employed the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) to define precise inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table \ref{tab:pico}).

\subsection{Analysis of Model Architectures}


Our analysis reveals a clear partitioning of MARL approaches according to structural complexity. The dominant category is Non-Hierarchical MARL, which represents 84.0\% ($N=63$) of the reviewed studies, mainly implemented through the Centralized Training and Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm. In contrast, Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) appears in only 16.0\% ($N=12$) of the corpus, despite its potential to handle multi-scale planning and sparse temporal rewards that are intrinsic to resource management.

Within the non-hierarchical group, the literature further divides into two main algorithmic families: \textbf{Value Decomposition Methods} and \textbf{Policy Gradient Extensions}.

Value Decomposition approaches, exemplified by QMIX \cite{rashidQMIXMonotonicValue2018a}, are often used for cooperative resource management. For instance, \cite{rayyan-353821111} employs an action-branching QMIX to optimize UAV trajectories and energy consumption, while \cite{rayyan-353822176} coordinates electric vehicle charging to reduce grid peaks. In both cases, decomposing the global Q-function enables agents to learn cooperative strategies for shared objectives.

Policy Gradient Extensions include Actor-Critic algorithms adapted from single-agent RL. The most common is MADDPG \cite{loweMultiAgentActorCriticMixed2020}, frequently applied to resource allocation in MEC and UAV networks \cite{rayyan-353821831}. Several studies propose variants such as FM-MADDPG for UAV swarm search tasks \cite{rayyan-353820459}. Other works rely on PPO-based frameworks integrated with Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to encode spatial dependencies in dynamic scheduling problems \cite{rayyan-353821868}. MADDPG and MAPPO \cite{yuSurprisingEffectivenessPPO2022} have become standard baselines for testing architectural innovations, as seen in \cite{rayyan-353822239}, which introduces a transformer-based model for a virtual power plant with a fluctuating number of agents.

The smaller HRL subset provides direct solutions for multi-scale control. These studies typically adopt a two-tier structure in which a high-level manager defines goals for lower-level workers, following the Feudal paradigm \cite{dayanFeudalReinforcementLearning1992}. Examples include hierarchical coordination in traffic signal control \cite{rayyan-353822005}, community energy management \cite{rayyan-353822026}, and city-wide ride-hailing systems that exploit spatial hierarchies \cite{rayyan-353821405}. These works demonstrate the potential of HRL architectures to manage temporal abstraction and coordination complexity, yet their limited adoption highlights a major research gap for real-world SES modeling.

From a broader perspective, the predominance of non-hierarchical MARL suggests that most current architectures remain optimized for short-horizon, well-structured environments. This imbalance limits their generalizability to SES, which are inherently multi-scale and partially observable. Addressing this architectural bias requires not only new design principles but also new experimental frameworks capable of evaluating scalability, data requirements, and human-in-the-loop adaptability.

\subsection{Analysis of Experimental Framework}

Our review reveals a strong concentration of MARL applications in infrastructural resource management. Transportation (30.7\%) and Energy Systems (24.0\%) together represent more than half of the reviewed studies, followed by UAV or Drone Systems (17.3\%) and Network or Cloud Resource Management (14.7\%). In contrast, applications in non-urban SES remain extremely limited. Only one study explicitly addressed agriculture \cite{Xiong20253075}, focusing on UAV-based data collection rather than human decision-making processes.

This disciplinary imbalance likely reflects the accessibility of standardized datasets and the availability of sectoral funding in urban and energy domains, as opposed to the data scarcity and higher heterogeneity of ecological contexts. As a result, adaptive MARL frameworks remain underexplored for modeling complex human-in-the-loop SES dynamics, an area that offers significant potential for methodological innovation.

Regarding data sources, approximately 45\% of the reviewed papers employed real-world datasets or production traces for validation. This indicates a growing trend toward empirical, problem-oriented evaluation beyond synthetic simulations.

\subsection{Analysis of Performance and Applicability}

Regarding performance, most studies emphasize system-level objectives, where the design of the reward function plays a decisive role. In non-hierarchical CTDE frameworks, global or team-based rewards are predominant. For instance, \cite{rayyan-353822176} trains agents to maximize a team reward for grid peak shaving, while \cite{rayyan-353821831} optimizes a global objective related to task offloading. Hierarchical studies, in contrast, explicitly decompose reward structures. Examples include \cite{rayyan-353822026}, which balances grid stability with prosumer cost reduction, and \cite{rayyan-353822005}, where a manager agent optimizes overall traffic flow while lower-level agents handle local intersections. Such decomposition directly mitigates the temporal credit assignment problem typical of long-horizon SES dynamics.

Performance evaluation is consistently benchmarked against baseline approaches. These baselines range from simple heuristics (e.g., random allocation) to single-agent RL models (SADDPG) \cite{rayyan-353821831} and established MARL algorithms such as MADDPG and MAPPO \cite{rayyan-353820459, rayyan-353822239}. The most frequent metrics fall into two categories: algorithmic (e.g., convergence speed, normalized reward) and system-level indicators specific to each domain, such as delay reduction, QoS satisfaction, or cost minimization.

In terms of applicability, the most advanced frameworks explicitly address scalability, robustness, and generalization. Scalability is often achieved by integrating Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for large-scale scheduling \cite{rayyan-353821868} or by using geographical hierarchies in HRL for city-wide coordination \cite{rayyan-353821405}. Robustness to dynamic environments remains a major concern. Several studies test their models under changing conditions \cite{rayyan-353821111} \textbf{add citations}.

Overall, while performance reporting is systematic, evaluation practices remain fragmented and often domain-dependent. Few studies assess computational cost, transferability, or interpretability, which are critical dimensions for deploying MARL–ABM frameworks in real-world SES applications.

\section{Research Direction}
\label{research_directions}

Our systematic review reveals two critical, intersecting gaps: a methodological gap and a domain-specific gap. First, we observed a methodological scarcity of Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), which appeared in only 16.0\% of studies. This underrepresentation suggests that the non-hierarchical CTDE frameworks dominating the literature (84.0\%) may not fully capture the multi-scale planning and delayed reward structures characteristic of complex resource management. Second, we identified a domain gap, reflected in the limited number of applications addressing non-urban Socio-Ecological Systems (SES), particularly agriculture. These two gaps appear closely related. The complex, multi-scale, and long-horizon dynamics of SES, such as Sahelian agro-sylvo-pastoral systems, require precisely the kind of temporal and strategic abstraction that HRL provides.

Building on this diagnosis, our research proposes a hybrid, hierarchical framework tailored to this challenge. This approach aligns with the “Macro-ABM decision-making” scenario identified by \cite{Zhang20232170}, in which Machine Learning operates at the system level to support high-level governance and policy optimization. The objective is to design a simulation platform where a macro-level “policymaker” agent interacts dynamically with a spatially explicit ABM (Fig.~\ref{fig:abm_rl}).

In this framework, the macro-agent, trained via Reinforcement Learning, operationalizes this “macro-decision-making” role. It acts on a slow timescale, learning optimal long-term carbon-capture and resource-management strategies at the territorial level. The micro-agents (e.g., farmers, herders) operate on a faster timescale within the ABM, following cognitively plausible behavioral rules such as a Belief–Desire–Intention (BDI) architecture \cite{bratmanIntentionPlansPractical1987}. This HRL-ABM hybrid allows for the systematic evaluation of carbon-capture policies under uncertainty while remaining computationally tractable by streamlining the state and action spaces for the high-level learning agent.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{conclusion}

This paper presented a systematic review of the literature at the intersection of Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) and Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), guided by the PRISMA protocol. Our primary objective was to critically assess the methodological landscape of MARL applied to decentralized resource management. Our analysis of the final corpus ($N=75$ articles) provided a quantitative diagnosis of the field’s maturity. The contribution of this paper lies in quantifying the methodological and structural gaps that impede real-world deployment. Specifically, we documented a pronounced reliance on Non-Hierarchical MARL (84\% of articles) despite the theoretical need for multi-scale planning, and identified a severe 'Domain Maturity Gap' due to the under-application of MARL to non-urban SES (e.g., agriculture and water management).

These documented gaps confirm that the dominant "full MARL" paradigm currently faces significant barriers in addressing the complexities of real-world environmental management. Our analysis thus concludes that Hierarchical and Hybrid architectures, which combine high-level strategic learning agents with lower-level agent-based models, represent the most fruitful and necessary research direction. This approach offers a tangible pathway toward developing the robust, scalable, and computationally tractable decision-support tools needed for achieving long-term sustainability goals.
